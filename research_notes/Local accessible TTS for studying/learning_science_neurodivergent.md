# Learning Science of TTS and Listening-Based Study for Neurodivergent Learners (ADHD, Autism, Dyslexia)

Scope note: Evidence gathered via web search on 2026-10-25 (about 17 searches). Many findings come from abstracts, evidence-map summaries, or search-result snippets rather than full texts; this is flagged where relevant. Items marked "[training knowledge, unverified this session]" are well-known claims that the researcher could not re-confirm against a live source (for example, w3.org was unreachable from this environment). The report writer should treat those as lower confidence.

Evidence strength legend used below: **STRONG** = meta-analysis or multiple controlled studies; **MODERATE** = single well-designed controlled studies or consistent smaller studies; **WEAK** = small-N, single case, qualitative, vendor or blog claims.

## Q1. Does TTS / bimodal reading (listening while reading, synchronized highlighting) improve comprehension, attention, or reading speed for ADHD, autism, dyslexia?

### Takeaway
TTS gives a small-to-moderate comprehension benefit for students with reading disabilities (Wood et al. 2018, d ≈ 0.35; read-aloud accommodations on reading tests ≈ 0.56 SD). For general readers, listening while reading adds only a trivial benefit (g = .18), and that benefit comes almost entirely from **externally paced** presentation (g = .41) rather than self-paced reading (g = .06). Evidence specific to ADHD is thin but positive. I found no outcome studies specific to autism, and no study that isolates synchronized highlighting.

### Cited Findings
- **STRONG.** Wood, Moxley, Tighe & Wagner (2018), *Journal of Learning Disabilities* 51(1):73–84. In this meta-analysis of TTS/read-aloud for students with reading disabilities (22 studies per the ASHA evidence map), the random-effects average weighted effect size on reading comprehension was **0.35 (95% CI 0.14–0.56, p < .01)**. Study design explained part of the variance, and the authors called for more research on moderators. — [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/8f7b9dd3-41fb-48c6-86a5-3e25ad47bc7b); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494021/)
- **STRONG (testing context).** Buzick & Stone (2014), "A Meta-Analysis of Research on the Read Aloud Accommodation," *Educational Measurement: Issues & Practice* 33(3):17–30. It covered K–12 studies from 1998–2013, with students with and without disabilities. On reading assessments, scores rose by an average of **0.56 SD** with read-aloud; the gain on math was much smaller (**~0.13**). A separate abstract reports a larger effect when a human reads rather than audio or computer players, but that link to this paper was not verified. — [ETS publication page](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/article/2014/jtbv.html); [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/527e5ed2-1fd9-4abb-97fe-a9e7f93978d9)
- **STRONG (general population).** Clinton-Lisell (2023), a systematic review and meta-analysis of reading while listening (RWL) versus reading only. It pooled 30 studies (N = 1,945, 62 effect sizes). The overall comprehension benefit was trivial, **g = .18**. The benefit was limited to **experimenter-paced** reading (**g = .41**) and absent when reading was **self-paced** (**g = .06**). Struggling readers "may be benefited," but there were too few studies to generalize. — [ERIC full text EJ1403866](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1403866.pdf); [UND Scholarly Commons](https://commons.und.edu/ehb-fac/75)
- **STRONG.** Clinton-Lisell's companion 2021 meta-analysis (Review of Educational Research, 46 studies) compared reading with listening. Overall comprehension did not reliably differ, but **reading had an advantage when readers were self-paced**. Per search-result summaries, this was attributed to readers' ability to regulate and look back. — [UND Scholarly Commons](https://commons.und.edu/ehb-fac/75) (via search summary)
- **MODERATE (ADHD, adolescents).** A Taiwan eye-tracking study with junior high students found that TTS **significantly improved comprehension for students with ADHD-related reading difficulties**, but it did not help the dyslexia group. — [NTNU Scholar record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/the-effects-of-text-to-speech-on-reading-comprehension-in-junior-/)
- **MODERATE (ADHD).** An RCT of AI-assisted audio-learning reported that students with greater ADHD symptom severity benefited particularly from the audio-learning modules. This comes from a search snippet only, and the details were not verified. — [UvA DARE record](https://dare.uva.nl/id/918af398-e739-4427-9cd7-448153dca2ef?page=519)
- **WEAK (ADHD, higher education, qualitative).** A Lund University study used focus groups and interviews on how students with ADHD use audiobooks and TTS in higher education. It notes that "little research exists" on actual use by students with ADHD. Its findings were not retrievable. — [Lund University LUP record](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/67f3db85-dfb1-48af-ba43-a7c32331d253)
- **WEAK (dyslexia, college).** A Hong Kong study of 43 college students (dyslexic and typical) compared three reading modes, including auditory TTS. Its main finding concerned reading **speed** rather than comprehension; details were not verified. — [HKU repository](https://repository.hku.hk/handle/10722/316509)
- **WEAK (seminal, dyslexia).** Elkind, Cohen & Murray (1993) studied middle schoolers with dyslexia using bimodal (synthesized speech plus highlighted text) presentation. A vendor summary reports that about 70% increased comprehension. The source is ReadSpeaker, a TTS vendor, so it has a conflict of interest. — [ReadSpeaker white paper](https://assets-www.readspeaker.com/uploads/ReadSpeaker_The_Benefits_of_Bimodal_Content_Presentation.pdf)
- **Context.** Georgiou et al. (2022) meta-analysed 76 studies and found that dyslexic readers show comprehension deficits relative to age-matched peers (g = 1.43) and to reading-level-matched peers (g = 0.64). These deficits are attributed to a combination of decoding and oral-language weaknesses. This explains why audio support, which bypasses decoding, should help partially but not fully. — cited within [Clinton-Lisell 2023, ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1403866.pdf) (via search summary)
- **WEAK (postsecondary).** Bruno et al. (2021) found improved comprehension for postsecondary students with intellectual and developmental disabilities after **direct instruction in how to use TTS**. — [Canvas community summary](https://community.canvaslms.com/t5/Accessibility/Research-on-Text-to-Speech-in-College-Classes/m-p/526333)

### Inferences
- The best-supported mechanism for a study app is **external pacing**. Audio drives the reader forward, which appears to be where RWL gains come from (g = .41 vs .06). A read-aloud app with synchronized highlighting naturally provides this. For ADHD users especially, it plausibly reduces drift and regressions.
- Because self-paced reading wins when learners can regulate, the app should still offer **easy look-back and replay controls**: replay the sentence, jump back one paragraph, pause on tap. Pure forced pacing could cost the regulation benefit.
- Expected gains are modest, roughly 0.2–0.4 SD. The app should not market TTS alone as transformative; the larger gains likely come from pairing it with segmentation and retrieval practice (Q3, Q5).
- Read-aloud gains on math tests were small (~0.13). This suggests that audio rendering of quantitative content needs special handling (Q7).
- Teaching users how to use features (onboarding) may matter (Bruno 2021).

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis or controlled study isolating **synchronized word/sentence highlighting** versus audio without highlighting. Highlighting granularity (word vs sentence) is untested in the sources I found.
- I found **no autism-specific TTS comprehension outcome studies**.
- ADHD evidence in **university** populations is almost entirely qualitative. The Taiwan study involved adolescents.
- Effects on reading **speed** and **attention** (as opposed to comprehension) were not quantified in meta-analytic form in the sources retrieved.

## Q2. Speech rate: optimal WPM, effects of 1.5x–2x, individual preference, ADHD preference for faster speech

### Takeaway
No rigorous study establishes an optimal TTS rate for learners with LD, ADHD, or autism. About 150 wpm (range 140–180) is the commonly cited default. Studies of speeded lecture video find little cost up to 1.5x and mixed evidence at 2x, with clear decline beyond 2x. Claims that ADHD users prefer faster speech are anecdotal. Defaults should be moderate, with user-adjustable rate and memory of the chosen setting.

### Cited Findings
- **WEAK–MODERATE.** "A rate between 140–180 words per minute" is described as optimal for students, citing Cunningham (2003, 2011). This is a secondary recommendation, and blogs repeat it rather than independently confirming it. — [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/article/text-speech-tts)
- **MODERATE (adjacent population).** In a 2019/2020 AJSLP study of 25 adults with aphasia, TTS at 113, 154, and 200 wpm was tested. People with aphasia as a group most **preferred and were most efficient at ~150 wpm**, with **significant individual differences**. — [ASHA AJSLP](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2019_AJSLP-19-00047)
- **WEAK.** A 2014 study compared TTS rates for 4 postsecondary students with reading difficulties, some with comorbid ADHD. The rate results could not be retrieved. — [Sagamore LDMJ](https://js.sagamorepub.com/index.php/ldmj/article/view/5278)
- **MODERATE.** Murphy, Hoover, Agadzhanyan, Kuehn & Castel (2022), *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, studied undergraduates watching lecture videos. They found **minimal costs from 1x to 1.5x or 2x**, but **performance declined beyond 2x**. Watching twice at 2x did not help, but **rewatching at 2x right before a test beat a single 1x viewing a week earlier**. Pausing and note-taking were not allowed. The authors caution that effects may differ with speech rate, complexity, and difficulty. — [Castel lab PDF](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf)
- **MODERATE (conflicting).** A later meta-analysis of 24 lecture-video studies reportedly found **minor effects up to 1.5x but a moderate-to-large detrimental effect at 2x or faster**. The authors were not identified in the search results. This contradicts Murphy et al. on 2x. — [Gigazine summary, 2025](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20250703-video-lecture-faster-speeds-disadvantages)
- **WEAK–MODERATE.** A scoping review of speech speed in audiovisual resources for university students found that four articles showed learning improvement at speeds **up to 2x**, while rates **greater than 2x limit learning**. — [CoDAS / SciELO scoping review](https://www.scielo.br/j/codas/a/bPThRNyMyKLGwJnvzdgh9tx/?lang=en)
- **WEAK (anecdotal).** Vendor and blog claims say ADHD listeners focus better slightly above normal speed, "1.25x–1.75x," with no study cited. — [AudioRead blog](https://audioread.com/help/is-text-to-speech-good-for-adhd-what-the-research-says); [Audeus vendor page](https://www.audeus.com/use-cases/adhd)
- **Context, synthetic speech.** Synthetic speech "requires longer time to process the meaning" than natural speech (review cited in the JACET study). This implies that the safe maximum speed may be lower for synthetic than for human voices. — [JACET Journal 53 (Tsukuba repository)](https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/48586/files/JacetJ_53_1.pdf)

### Inferences
- Recommended default: **~150–170 wpm (1.0x on most engines)** for first-pass study of dense science notes. Offer 0.75x–2.0x in fine steps (0.05–0.1x) and **remember per-user (and possibly per-document-type) speed**.
- Offer a **"review mode"** that suggests faster playback (1.5x–2x) for **second-pass** review of familiar material, consistent with Murphy's finding that 2x rewatching before a test helps. Dense first exposure should stay near 1x.
- Because 2x evidence is mixed and synthetic speech is harder to process, consider a **soft warning or nudge above ~1.75x for first-time content**, rather than a hard cap.
- Speed changes should preserve the pause durations at structural boundaries (see Q3). Engines that compress pauses proportionally at high speed may remove the segmentation cues the learner needs. This is an inference, not tested.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study of speech rate and comprehension in ADHD, autistic, or dyslexic **adults** using TTS.
- I found no study of rate × content difficulty (for example, science notes vs narrative).
- The 2x meta-analysis author was not identified, and its full text was not obtained.

## Q3. Prosody, pauses, natural vs synthetic voices, and segmentation (cognitive load theory; Mayer's segmenting and signaling principles)

### Takeaway
Synthetic speech is consistently harder to process and more effortful than natural speech, though the gap shrinks with exposure and modern neural voices likely narrow it further (untested in these sources). Segmenting (learner-paced chunks) has one of the largest effects in multimedia learning (d ≈ 0.70–0.98). Signaling (cues to structure) has a reliable small-to-moderate effect (d ≈ 0.38–0.46). Both map directly onto TTS design through pauses at boundaries, spoken headings, and "continue" points.

### Cited Findings
- **MODERATE.** Listening to and comprehending synthetic voices is more difficult than natural voices, but the difficulty **decreases with exposure** to the synthetic voice (1998 paper). — [UCF STARS thesis record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/etd/1813) (via search summary)
- **MODERATE.** Simantiraki et al. (Interspeech 2018) found that subjective effort ratings and intelligibility ranked in inverse order across speech types. **Synthetic speech was the most demanding**, and **pupillometry showed significantly more effort for synthetic speech** at the most favourable noise level. — [ISCA Archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2018/simantiraki18_interspeech.html)
- **MODERATE.** A 2023 follow-up (Frontiers in Neuroscience) tested native and non-native listeners. Enhanced speech was most intelligible and least effortful, and synthetic speech was the reverse. The relationship between intelligibility and processing demand is "mediated by speech type." — [Frontiers in Neuroscience 2023](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2023.1235911/text)
- **WEAK–MODERATE.** In a JACET study (EFL learners), natural speech was comprehended better than synthetic, and **prior exposure to synthetic speech affected comprehensibility**. The authors still support using synthetic speech for learning materials. — [JACET Journal (Tsukuba)](https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/48586/files/JacetJ_53_1.pdf)
- **STRONG.** For the segmenting principle, Mayer's Cambridge Handbook chapter reports it was supported in **10 of 10 experimental tests, median d = 0.79**. Another review gives **d = 0.70**. Mayer's segmenting chapter also cites **d = 0.98** for problem-solving transfer, but that comes from 3 tests on one narrated animation. — [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258); [Mayer, Multimedia Learning — Segmenting principle](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/multimedia-learning/segmenting-principle/37240877DDA0362355ADB39936027982)
- **STRONG.** A 2020 meta-analysis of the signaling principle in *ETR&D* pooled 29 experiments (44 effect sizes, N = 2,726) and found **d = .38**. The effect was larger in higher-quality studies that controlled for prior knowledge. Another review reports **d = 0.46**. — [WSU Research Exchange record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842)
- **MODERATE (math prosody).** Gellenbeck & Stefik (2009) found that **inserting pauses dramatically improved disambiguation** of spoken algebraic expressions. The study had 16 CS students, and its target audience was learners with LD. — via [ETS / a11y review summaries](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69db92073bb6aaff67a0cd3a)
- **WEAK–MODERATE.** Souza & Freitas found that inappropriate pauses and long uninterrupted auditory runs make memorization harder in synthesized math speech for visually impaired students. They proposed a pause model. — [SBIE paper](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbie/article/view/22458)

### Inferences
- **Pauses as signaling.** Use hierarchical, longer pauses at larger structural boundaries: sentence < paragraph < bullet list end < subsection < section. Read headings aloud with a distinct cue, such as a brief earcon or a slight pitch or voice change, so structure is audible. This applies the signaling principle (d ≈ 0.4) to audio. The exact pause durations are design choices, not established by research here; consider sentence ≈ 300–500 ms, paragraph ≈ 700–1000 ms, and section ≈ 1.5–2 s with a cue, all user-adjustable.
- **Segmenting.** Break playback into learner-paced segments, such as one subsection or concept. Optionally **auto-pause at section ends** and wait for "continue" (segmenting d ≈ 0.7–0.8 comes from *learner-paced* segments). This also reconciles the external pacing benefit within a segment (Q1) with learner control between segments.
- **Voice quality.** Use the most natural, highest-quality neural voice available locally, because synthetic speech raises listening effort and fatigue. Because effort drops with exposure, **keep a consistent default voice** rather than rotating voices.
- Consider a **"warm-up" or familiarization** effect: new users may comprehend less in the first sessions with a synthetic voice.

### Gaps
- No studies retrieved compare **modern neural TTS** (2020+) with human speech on comprehension or fatigue in neurodivergent learners. Most synthetic-speech findings predate neural TTS.
- Optimal pause durations at structural boundaries for comprehension have not been empirically established in the sources found.
- No ADHD-, autism-, or dyslexia-specific studies of segmenting or signaling in audio-only presentation were found.

## Q4. Autism-specific considerations: sensory sensitivity to voice timbre, predictability, monotone vs expressive voices, auditory processing

### Takeaway
Decreased sound tolerance (hyperacusis and misophonia) is common in autism, with estimates of roughly 27–60% depending on the measure. This makes **volume, timbre, and harshness control** and avoidance of sudden sounds important. Direct evidence on autistic preference for monotone vs expressive or synthetic vs human voices is essentially absent. The "autistic people prefer monotone or robotic voices" idea is not supported by research found here.

### Cited Findings
- **STRONG.** Williams, Suzman & Woynaroski (2021) meta-analysis of hyperacusis in autism: current prevalence was **41.42%** and lifetime **60.58%** by interview or questionnaire; current prevalence was **27.30%** by objective or observational measures. Heterogeneity was high, and prevalence in ADI-R studies rose with age. — [PMC8349927](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349927)
- **MODERATE.** In an adult study (Vanderbilt dissertation, n = 936 autistic adults), clinically significant hyperacusis was found in **40.2%**, misophonia in **35.5%**, and both in **27.0%**. — [Vanderbilt IR](https://ir.vanderbilt.edu/items/a9e54a3e-61db-467b-b2cf-2eb0e9f8b977)
- **MODERATE.** Psychoacoustic estimates are lower: 18% (80 dB HL clicks) and **37% using speech loudness discomfort levels**. Survey estimates in children reach 77.6% current. — [BAA scoping review poster](https://www.baaudiology.org/app/uploads/2022/10/57-Decreased-Sound-Tolerance-in-Autism-Spectrum-Disorder-A-Scoping-Review.pdf); [PMC7855558](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7855558/)
- **MODERATE.** A 2016 *Scientific Reports* study found that autistic adults responded **faster** than neurotypical adults to voices versus non-voice sounds, which counters the assumption of reduced voice attention. — [PMC4872056](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4872056)
- **MODERATE (seminal, children).** Kuhl et al. (2005, *Developmental Science*) found that autistic preschoolers as a group preferred non-speech sounds over "motherese" (highly expressive) speech. — [PubMed 15647058](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15647058)
- **MODERATE.** A 2024 *Frontiers in Psychiatry* study used synthetic speech to convey uncertainty cues. Autistic and neurotypical adults both detected the uncertainty levels, and autistic adults rated stimuli as slightly (not significantly) less uncertain. This suggests prosodic cues in synthetic speech are perceivable by autistic adults. — [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2024.1347913/pdf)
- **WEAK.** An AAC study of 4 non-verbal autistic children comparing digitized and synthetic speech output found mixed results and individual preferences. — [UT Austin repository](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/handle/2152/21288)

### Inferences
- **Sensory comfort is a first-class requirement.** Provide a volume ceiling and normalization (no loudness jumps between voices or segments), optional mild treble roll-off or "soft" voice options to reduce sibilance and harshness, and **no unexpected sounds**. Any earcons or chimes at boundaries must be optional, quiet, low-frequency, and toggleable.
- **Predictability.** Keep a consistent voice, consistent pause patterns, and consistent behavior at boundaries. Announce structure ("Section 2 of 5: ...") so the listener knows what is coming. This aligns with COGA's predictable-design guidance (Q6).
- **Expressiveness as a user choice.** Because evidence is individual and mixed (children's aversion to exaggerated motherese; adults can perceive synthetic prosody), offer an expressiveness or prosody setting (calm/neutral vs expressive) rather than choosing for the user. A **calm, moderately expressive** default seems prudent: preserve the prosody that marks structure, and avoid dramatic, sales-like delivery.
- Let users preview voices before committing, and offer several timbres (lower and higher pitch).

### Gaps
- I found no study testing autistic adults' comprehension or comfort with monotone vs expressive TTS, or with synthetic vs human voices for study material.
- I found no research linking specific acoustic features (pitch, sibilance, breathiness) to discomfort in autistic listeners for speech, as opposed to environmental sounds.
- Auditory processing differences in autism (for example, speech in noise) were not covered in depth in the sources retrieved.

## Q5. Active-learning integration: retrieval-practice pauses, self-explanation prompts, spaced review in audio

### Takeaway
Strong evidence shows that interpolating brief memory tests into lectures halves mind-wandering and improves retention (Szpunar et al. 2013). This is directly relevant to ADHD attention problems and supports building optional quiz pauses into audio playback.

### Cited Findings
- **MODERATE–STRONG.** Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013), *PNAS*, ran 2 experiments (about 80 students per press summaries). Interpolating online lectures with memory tests **reduced off-task mind wandering, increased note-taking, and improved learning**. It also **reduced anxiety** about a final cumulative test and **lowered subjective cognitive demand**. — [PMC3631699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/); [Harvard Psychology](https://psychology.fas.harvard.edu/publications/interpolated-memory-tests-reduce-mind-wandering-and-improve-learning-online)
- **MODERATE.** Szpunar reported that tested students in a 21-minute statistics lecture were **about half as likely to mind-wander**, **3x as likely to take additional notes**, and better at retaining content. Untested students reported mind-wandering about **40%** of the time. — [MedicalResearch.com interview](https://medicalresearch.com/interpolated-memory-tests-reduce-mind-wandering-and-improve-learning-of-online-lectures/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/04/130404122240.htm)
- **MODERATE.** Jing, Szpunar & Schacter (2016) extended interpolated testing to focused attention and information integration in video lectures. — cited in [PMC3631699 related literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/) (via search summary)
- **MODERATE.** Murphy et al. (2022) found that re-exposure at 2x right before a test improved performance over a single earlier 1x exposure, which is consistent with spacing and review benefits. — [Castel lab PDF](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf)
- **[training knowledge, unverified this session]** The testing effect and spacing effect are among the most robust findings in learning science. Dunlosky et al. (2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) rated practice testing and distributed practice as "high utility." Rowland (2014, *Psychological Bulletin*) meta-analysis reported a testing effect of g ≈ 0.50. Self-explanation was rated "moderate utility" by Dunlosky et al.

### Inferences
- Add an optional **"check yourself" pause** at the end of each segment or section, for example every ~5–10 minutes of audio or each subsection. The pause asks 1–2 short recall questions (generated from headings or key terms) before continuing. It should be optional, skippable, and non-punitive, with no loud error sounds (see Q4).
- Questions should require **recall** ("What are the two products of glycolysis?") rather than recognition, and should give immediate feedback by replaying the relevant sentence.
- Support **spaced review**: an end-of-session summary, a "review queue" of earlier sections, and a faster-playback review mode for previously studied material.
- For ADHD, the mind-wandering reduction is the key mechanism. Interpolated prompts likely matter more than voice or speed tweaks.

### Gaps
- I found no studies specifically testing interpolated questions in **audio-only** TTS playback, or in ADHD, autistic, or dyslexic samples.
- I found no evidence on the optimal **frequency** of interpolated questions for audio.
- Self-explanation prompts in audio contexts were not found in this search.

## Q6. Accessibility guidelines: W3C/WAI COGA, BDA Dyslexia Style Guide, CAST UDL

### Takeaway
COGA's "Making Content Usable" note and the BDA style guide converge on clear structure, short chunks, plain language, avoiding memory load, predictability, and user personalization. These are design-level guidance, not experimental evidence, but they reflect expert and user consensus.

### Cited Findings
- **Guidance.** W3C *Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities* is a W3C Working Group Note from the COGA Task Force. It is supplemental to WCAG and not required for conformance. It covers objectives, design patterns, user research and testing, personas, and user needs. Each pattern describes user needs, what to do, how it helps, and examples. — [W3C TR coga-usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable)
- **Guidance.** Its objectives include helping users find what they need, **using clear content** (easy words, **short sentences and blocks of text**), helping users avoid mistakes, and **ensuring processes do not rely on memory**. A secondary source summarizes the guidance as favoring familiar design patterns with content "in manageable chunks." — [W3C TR coga-usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable); [Deque axe-con 2022 COGA slides](https://www.deque.com/axe-con/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/axe-con-2022-COGA-Content-Usable.pdf)
- **[training knowledge, unverified this session; w3.org unreachable]** The full set of COGA objectives is: (1) help users understand what things are and how to use them; (2) help users find what they need; (3) use clear content (text, images, media); (4) help users avoid mistakes and know how to correct them; (5) **help users focus** (minimize distractions and interruptions); (6) ensure processes do not rely on memory; (7) provide help and support; (8) **support adaptation and personalization**; (9) test with real users. Relevant patterns include "Use a consistent visual design," "Make each step clear," "Avoid distractions," "Provide alternatives to text (media/audio)," and "Make short text chunks." Verify exact names against the live document.
- **Guidance.** The British Dyslexia Association *Dyslexia Style Guide (2023)* recommends sans-serif fonts (Arial, Verdana, Calibri; Comic Sans also listed), **bullet points over continuous prose**, clear instructions, no double negatives, limited abbreviations, and a glossary for jargon. It is meant to be used alongside WCAG and to support text-to-speech use. — [BDA guide copy hosted by BU](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx); [Ako Aotearoa copy](https://ako.ac.nz/assets/Knowledge-centre/ALNACC-Resources/Dyslexia-resources/230907-Dyslexia-Friendly-Style-Guide.pdf)
- **Guidance (2018 edition).** The earlier edition covered fonts, headings and structure, colour, layout, and writing style, and also advised avoiding pure white backgrounds and reducing glare. — [Wigan Council PDF](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf); [Creative Store summary](https://thecreativestore.co.nz/creative-talk/design/designing-for-dyslexia-a-style-guide/)
- **[training knowledge, unverified this session]** CAST UDL Guidelines (v3.0, 2024) call for providing multiple means of representation, including "offer alternatives for auditory and visual information" and "customize the display of information." They also call for supporting executive functions and sustaining attention by minimizing threats and distractions. — [CAST UDL Guidelines](https://udlguidelines.cast.org)

### Inferences
- Visual side of bimodal reading: use a sans-serif font, adjustable size and spacing, an off-white or cream background option, and high but not glaring contrast (BDA). Highlighting should be clearly visible but not flashy, and preferably adjustable in colour.
- **Personalization is explicitly endorsed** (COGA objective, UDL). Every parameter (speed, pauses, voice, expressiveness, highlight style, quiz frequency) should be user-adjustable with sensible defaults, and settings should persist.
- "Do not rely on memory": always show where the user is (section X of Y, progress bar), allow resuming exactly where they left off, and keep controls consistent.
- "Help users focus": a minimal, distraction-free playback screen, and no autoplay of unrelated content or notifications.

### Gaps
- The full COGA pattern list and the BDA 2023 PDF text could not be retrieved directly in this session. Exact pattern names should be verified.
- I found no CHADD- or Understood.org-specific TTS design guidance in this search (not searched in depth).

## Q7. Auditory presentation of formulas, tables, and diagrams

### Takeaway
For math, research favors **natural-language speech styles with prosodic pauses** (ClearSpeak) over verbose lexical-marker styles (MathSpeak) for most notations. Pauses alone substantially improve disambiguation. MathSpeak remains useful when unambiguous structure is critical. For tables, explicit **row and column structure cues** help listeners more than auditory embellishments. Evidence comes mostly from blind and low-vision or print-disabled users with small samples.

### Cited Findings
- **MODERATE.** ETS (Frankel & Brownstein, 2016), *Development and Initial Evaluation of the ClearSpeak Style for Automated Speaking of Algebra*: for the tested notations, **ClearSpeak was superior to SimpleSpeak and MathSpeak** in MathPlayer. ClearSpeak uses familiar language plus pauses. — [ETS report](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2016/jwog.html)
- **MODERATE.** ETS (2016), *An Evaluation of the Usefulness of Prosodic and Lexical Cues for Understanding Synthesized Speech of Mathematics*, evaluated how blind and low-vision students drew conclusions about expression content and structure, and their judgments of pace and wording. Full findings were not retrieved. — [ETS report](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2016/jwvb.html)
- **MODERATE.** Gellenbeck & Stefik (2009) found that **pauses alone dramatically improved disambiguation** of algebraic expressions, deliberately avoiding lexical cues like "begin fraction." — [a11y review summary](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69db92073bb6aaff67a0cd3a)
- **MODERATE.** Isaacson, Schleppenbach & Lloyd (2014) found MathSpeak efficacious for disambiguating spoken math among students with print disabilities. MathSpeak is a rule set for non-ambiguous rendering of MathML. — [Inderscience: MathSpeak](https://www.inderscience.com/offer.php?id=91609); [RIT JSESD](https://repository.rit.edu/jsesd/vol14/iss1/3)
- **WEAK–MODERATE.** Souza & Freitas found that inappropriate pauses and long uninterrupted auditory runs make memorization of spoken math harder. — [SBIE](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbie/article/view/22458)
- **MODERATE.** Read-aloud accommodations help math test scores much less than reading (≈0.13 vs 0.56 SD). — [ETS Buzick & Stone page](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/article/2014/jtbv.html)
- **WEAK–MODERATE.** In tables, a dissertation with 16 legally blind participants found that **spatial stereo panning** helped orientation within a 5×6 HTML table more than a centred fixed voice. — [FIU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.fiu.edu/etd/1570)
- **WEAK–MODERATE.** A review of user studies with blind participants found that the spatial and semantic organization of table information significantly affects navigation. **Adding row and column structure cues helped more than colour or auditory enhancements** when structure was absent. — [a11y review](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69dd42d40388a431b20437f5)
- **Context.** Chart Reader (Microsoft/UW, CHI 2023) was co-designed with 10 screen-reader users for chart and data access. It supports structured navigation rather than linear reading. — [Microsoft Research PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2023/04/ChartReader-CHI2023.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Formulas.** Default to a ClearSpeak-like natural rendering ("x squared plus 3x, all over 2") with **pauses marking grouping**. Offer a verbose or unambiguous mode (MathSpeak-like: "start fraction ... over ... end fraction") as an option. Slow down slightly and insert pauses around formulas, and offer "repeat formula" and "step through formula" controls. Chemistry notation (for example, subscripts and charges) needs custom rules. **Visually highlight the whole formula** while it is spoken.
- **Tables.** Avoid a long linear read-out. Announce the table first ("Table: 4 rows, 3 columns: Enzyme, Substrate, Product"), then read **row by row with column headers repeated** ("Row 1. Enzyme: amylase. Substrate: starch..."). Offer "skip table" and "explore table" options. Structure cues beat embellishment.
- **Diagrams.** Speak alt text or captions and announce "Figure: ..." with an option to skip. Long descriptions should be on demand rather than read by default, to limit working-memory load.
- Because math read-aloud gains are small, pair formulas with the **visual display** (bimodal) rather than relying on audio alone.

### Gaps
- I found no studies on audio rendering of math, tables, or diagrams for **sighted** neurodivergent learners (ADHD, autism, dyslexia). All evidence comes from blind, low-vision, or print-disabled users.
- No research was found on spoken chemical formulas or reaction equations.
- Full ETS 2016 results (prosodic vs lexical cues) were not obtained.
